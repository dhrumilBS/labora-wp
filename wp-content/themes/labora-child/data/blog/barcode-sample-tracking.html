    Why samples go missing

    Ask any lab manager about their worst day and a lost sample is usually part of the story. A doctor calls about an urgent result, the case shows the sample was collected, and nobody can say where the tube is now. Someone checks the refrigerators, someone else calls the collection center, and the patient may need to come back for a redraw.

    Samples rarely vanish. They sit in the wrong rack, wait in a transport box, or carry a handwritten label nobody can match to a case. The problem is not the tube. It is that the lab only knows where a sample was at registration, and loses track of it at every handoff after that.

    Barcode tracking fixes this by giving each tube one record that follows it through the lab. Every time someone scans it, the record learns where the tube is, who has it, and when it moved.

    Label at the point of collection

    The single most useful change is also the simplest: print the barcode label when the sample is drawn, at the front desk or the collection point, and stick it on the tube before it leaves the patient's side.

    Labels printed later, at the bench, depend on someone matching a tube to a request form from memory. That is where swapped samples and unlabeled tubes come from. A label printed at collection carries the patient, the tests, and the tube type from the start.

    If a label is damaged, print a new one from the same case and record why it was replaced. A tube relabeled by hand, with no trace, is exactly the gap barcode tracking is meant to close.

    What to scan, and when

    You do not need a scan for every movement in the building. You need one at each point where responsibility for the sample changes hands. For most diagnostic labs that is five or six scans:

    CollectedFront desk, collection center, or patient's homeThe sample exists, matched to the patient and tests
    ReceivedLab receptionTransport is over; the turnaround clock moves to the lab
    RackedDepartment benchExact rack and slot, so anyone can find it
    In testingInstrument or benchWork has started on the sample

    Each scan takes a second, and together they turn a guess into a timeline. When a doctor calls, the answer is on screen: received at 10:12, racked in Biochemistry rack 3, slot B4, in testing since 10:40.

    Give every tube a home

    A scan at receipt tells you a sample is in the lab. A scan into a rack slot tells you exactly where. That second step is what ends the search through refrigerators.

    Each tube's record shows every scan, who made it, and the rack and slot where the tube sits now.

    Rack maps work best when they match the physical racks on the bench, and when they are color-coded by tube type, so a technician can see at a glance whether an EDTA tube has ended up in a serum rack. Keep rack names short and printed on the rack itself, so the scan and the shelf always agree.

    Record every hold and rejection

    Some samples cannot be tested as they arrive: hemolyzed, clotted, insufficient, wrong tube, or missing information. What matters is what happens next. If a technician sets the tube aside and moves on, nobody outside the bench knows, and the report is simply late.

    Make rejecting a sample a scan with a reason. The case shows the rejection immediately, the front desk or the collection center can arrange a redraw the same day, and the reason is saved for later. Over a few weeks, the reasons tell you where to act: one collection center with frequent hemolysis may need retraining on draw technique, and a run of insufficient samples may point to the wrong tube sizes in a home collection kit.

    Mark urgent cases when the label is printed, so the urgent flag shows on every scan screen and the tube goes to the front of each queue without anyone having to remember. A 20-minute demo with your own tests and racks: scan a tube and follow it from registration to report.

    Rolling it out without slowing the lab

    Barcode tracking changes a habit at every desk, so introduce it in an order that keeps the lab running:

    1. Start with labels at collection. Even before any other scan, printed labels remove most identification errors.
    2. Add the receipt scan. It splits turnaround into transport time and lab time, which is often a revelation.
    3. Introduce racks one department at a time. Begin where searches happen most, usually biochemistry or hematology.
    4. Make rejection a scan. Once racks are in place, recording reasons is a small extra step.
    5. Review missed scans weekly. A tube that skipped a scan shows you which step is still being done the old way.

    What to review each week

    • Samples received without a collection scan, by center
    • Rejections by reason and by collection point
    • Average time from collection to receipt, by center
    • Tubes still in a rack after their report was signed
    • Any sample that took more than one search to find
