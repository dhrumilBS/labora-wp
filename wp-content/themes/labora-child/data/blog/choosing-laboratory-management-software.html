    Before you look at software

    The most common mistake in choosing laboratory management software is starting with the software. Vendors show what their system does well, and every demo looks good. Without a clear picture of your own lab, it is hard to tell which strengths matter to you.

    Spend an afternoon writing down how a case moves through your lab today: who registers the patient, where samples are collected, how they reach the bench, who validates and signs, and how reports reach patients and doctors. Note where the delays and errors happen. That list becomes your test for every vendor.

    Pick three real cases, for example a routine profile, an urgent test, and an imaging study with lab work. Ask each vendor to walk through those cases, from registration to delivered report.

    Registration and sample tracking

    • Can the front desk register a patient, tests, and payment on one screen?
    • Are barcode labels printed at the point of collection, including home visits?
    • Does every handoff get a scan, with the time and the person recorded?
    • Can you find any tube by rack and slot in seconds?
    • Are holds and rejections recorded with a reason, visible to the front desk at once?

    Turnaround time

    • Can you set targets per test and priority, not one number for the lab?
    • Does the team see cases at risk before they are late?
    • Can you see on-time rate by test, center, and phase?

    Reporting and sign-off

    • Do results flow into report templates without retyping?
    • Are pathology, radiology, and cardiology reports on the same patient case?
    • Can permissions control who signs which reports?
    • Are signed reports delivered to patients and referring doctors automatically?
    • Are changes after signing recorded as amendments, with the original kept?

    Multiple centers and partners

    If you run more than one center, or send samples to partner labs, ask how the system handles them. You want one view of the whole business, with each center's work and revenue still visible on its own.

    • Can staff switch between centers without separate logins?
    • Can samples move between centers and outsource labs with their history intact?
    • Are prices, staff, and permissions set per center where needed?

    Integrations

    Ask which instruments and systems the software connects with today, not which it could connect with. Get the list in writing.

    ConnectionQuestion to ask
    Lab instrumentsWhich of our analyzers send results directly, and what does it take to connect a new one?
    Billing and accountingHow do bills, payments, and dues reach our accounting tools?
    MessagingHow are reports and notifications sent to patients and doctors?
    Your own systemsIs there a documented API, and who supports it?

    Security and privacy

    A lab system holds some of the most sensitive data a patient has. Ask for specific answers, not general reassurance:

    • Does every person sign in as themselves, with access set by role and center?
    • Is there an audit trail of who viewed and changed each record?
    • How is data encrypted, backed up, and restored, and how often is restoring tested?
    • Who at the vendor can access your data, and under what agreement?
    • How are security issues reported and fixed?
    A vendor serious about security can share its security overview, the agreements it signs with customers, and its policies, before you sign.

    Data migration

    Your history matters: past patients, reports, price lists, and referrer records. Ask what will be moved, how it will be checked, and what happens to the old system afterwards.

    "Can you migrate our data?" "How long does migration take?" "Will our reports come over?" "Which records and years will you move, and how do we check them?" "Who does each migration task, and when do we test it?" "Can we see a sample of our migrated reports before go-live?"

    Rollout and support

    The best software can still fail in a rushed rollout. Ask for a plan with names and dates:

    1. Setup. Who configures tests, prices, templates, and permissions?
    2. Training. How is each role trained, and is there help on the first live days?
    3. Go-live. Can centers go live one at a time, with the old system available as a fallback?
    4. Support. How do you reach support, and how quickly do they answer during lab hours?

    References and the final decision

    Ask for references from labs similar to yours in size, departments, and number of centers, and ask them what surprised them after go-live. Then compare vendors on your own list, not on features you will not use. The right system is the one that makes your lab's day calmer, not the one with the longest feature sheet.

    Laboratory management software runs a diagnostic lab's daily work: patient registration and billing, sample tracking, results and validation, reporting and signatures, delivery to patients and doctors, and the numbers owners use to run the business. It depends on the number of centers, departments, and records to migrate. A plan with named tasks, a tested migration, and a staged go-live by center matters more than the total time.
