A lab rarely decides to replace its spreadsheets on a quiet day. Usually, something has started to hurt. A sample gets delayed because nobody can tell where it is. A report has to be rebuilt after someone finds a mismatch. The person who "knows how the spreadsheet works" is on vacation. Meanwhile, your team is busy, customers want results, and the backlog keeps growing.

That is when laboratory management software starts to look less like an IT purchase and more like a way to run the business.

For a growing U.S. testing lab, the right LIMS (Laboratory Information Management System) can bring sample tracking, test workflows, results, and reporting into a more connected process. The hard part is choosing a system that fits your lab's actual work. NIST describes LIMS functions that include specimen tracking, data collection, workflow annotation, and dataset aggregation.

Source: NIST Research Data Framework

The best choice usually is not the system with the longest feature list. It is the one your team can use to move work from intake to final report with fewer handoffs, fewer avoidable errors, and a clear record of what happened.

Start with the problem you are trying to solve

Before you compare software, follow a real sample through your lab. What happens when it arrives? Who logs it? How are tests assigned? Where do analysts record results? Who reviews them? How does the finished report reach the customer?

Now look for the places where the process depends on memory, repeated data entry, or a particular person being available. Those are useful clues about what the new system needs to handle.

A lab may say, "We need sample tracking," when the deeper issue is that staff cannot see which samples are waiting for review. Or it may ask for automation when the real problem is that results are copied between an instrument file, a spreadsheet, and a report.

Write the problem down in operational terms:

  • We spend too long assembling customer reports.
  • We cannot quickly see which samples are past their due date.
  • A change to a result is difficult to trace.
  • We enter the same customer or sample information in multiple places.
  • We cannot easily see workload by analyst or test.

These statements give you a way to evaluate software against the work that matters. They also help keep the project focused when a vendor shows polished features that do not address your bottlenecks.

Map the workflow you actually have

A demo can make almost any system look simple. Your day-to-day process is a better test. Map one common job from sample receipt through reporting. Include exceptions, too: a sample arrives without complete information, a customer changes the requested tests, a result needs review, or a test has to be repeated.

Ask vendors to show that workflow using your terminology and examples. Do not settle for a tour of menus. Ask them to demonstrate:

  • How a sample is registered and identified
  • How work is assigned and status updated
  • How results move from entry or instrument data to review
  • How a reviewer handles a correction
  • How the final report is generated
  • How someone can retrieve the sample history later

This matters because the cost of software is not just its subscription or license. It is also the effort required to make the system fit your processes, and the effort your team spends working around it.

Treat sample traceability as a daily operating need

Traceability is not only for an audit or a customer complaint. It helps answer basic questions while work is underway: Where is this sample? What has been done? What is waiting? Who owns the next step?

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency describes LIMS as supporting management and tracking of samples, instruments, standards, data transfer, and reporting.

Source: EPA Quality Assurance Handbook

When you evaluate a system, test how it handles the identifiers and events your lab depends on. Can staff see a sample's status without chasing someone down? Can the system connect a sample to its tests, results, and report? Can you retrieve the relevant history without searching through old files and email?

Ask the vendor to show how the system records changes, too. If someone edits a result or updates a record, what history is available, and who can see it? The answer should match your lab's procedures and obligations.

Check integrations before you assume they are included

"Integrates with your instruments" can mean several different things. It might mean the vendor has connected to that model before. It might mean a file can be imported. Or it might mean the connection still needs to be scoped, built, and checked for your setup.

Ask:

  • Which instruments and file formats are supported
  • Whether data transfer is automatic or still requires manual import
  • What happens when a transfer fails or a result does not match
  • Whether the interface is included in the quote
  • Who maintains it when an instrument or software version changes

Do the same for accounting, customer portals, ERP systems, or other tools your team needs to connect. A LIMS can centralize laboratory work, but it will not automatically make every surrounding system work together.

Make the compliance conversation specific to your lab

If your lab works with regulated records, ask what controls the system provides for access, record changes, retention, electronic signatures, and validation support. Then check those features against the regulations and procedures that apply to your operation.

Requirements depend on your work and the records involved. FDA guidance describes Part 11 in relation to electronic records maintained under FDA record requirements, and recommends a risk-based approach to validation decisions.

Source: FDA guidance on Part 11 scope and application

Ask the vendor for documentation you can review with your quality lead. Confirm what your team is responsible for configuring, validating, and maintaining. Software can support your quality processes; it cannot make those decisions for you.

Understand the whole cost, not just the quote

The license or subscription is one line in the budget. Before choosing, ask for a written breakdown of:

  • Setup and configuration
  • Data migration
  • Instrument and system integrations
  • Training
  • Validation documentation, if applicable
  • Ongoing support
  • Additional users, sites, storage, or modules

Also ask what happens after the initial rollout. Who can make routine changes? How are support issues handled? What happens if you add a test, change an instrument, or open another location?

A cheaper quote may be a good deal. It may also leave important work outside the scope. Find out which before you compare prices.

Plan implementation around a real lab week

A LIMS rollout changes how people do their work. That means implementation needs time from the people who know the process: analysts, supervisors, quality staff, and whoever handles intake and reporting.

Before signing, get clear on what the vendor will deliver, what your team must provide, how data will move, and how the system will be checked before launch. Pick a defined first workflow or group of tests if that makes the transition manageable. Do not launch every process at once just because the software can support it.

Ask how staff training works and how your team can get help after go-live. The handoff matters: a system that only one person knows how to use can leave the lab with a new dependency instead of a more dependable process.

Choose the system your team can run

A useful LIMS should help your lab answer everyday questions with less guesswork: What came in? What is in progress? What needs attention? Which result is ready for review? Has the customer received the report?

Make your decision based on your workflow, the workarounds you want to remove, and the full cost of getting the system into use. Bring the people who do the work into the evaluation, and ask vendors to show, not just tell, how the software handles a real sample from intake to report.

The right system will not fix every process problem by itself. But a well-chosen one can give your team a clearer, more consistent way to manage the work as the lab grows.
