    Why trust decides whether patients book again

    For many patients, a home collection visit is the most personal contact they will ever have with your lab. Someone comes into their home, often early in the morning, often when they are fasting or unwell. If the visit runs late, the instructions were unclear, or the report takes longer than a walk-in would have, they remember it.

    The good news is that trust in home collection is built from a few predictable moments: the booking, the reminder, the arrival, the draw, and the report. Get each one right and patients book again, and tell their families.

    Make booking and preparation clear

    Most failed visits start at booking. A patient who did not know they had to fast, or who booked a time the phlebotomist cannot reach, turns a visit into a redraw.

    • Offer time windows you can keep. A one-hour window that you meet is worth more than a precise time that you miss.
    • Send preparation with the confirmation. Fasting, timing for hormone tests, stopping certain medicines on a doctor's advice: tell patients when they book, not at the door.
    • Confirm the address and access. A gate code, a floor number, or a landmark saves more time than a faster route.
    "Do not eat or drink anything except water after 10 p.m." is clearer than "12-hour fasting required." Patients follow instructions they understand.

    Share a live arrival time

    The question every home collection patient asks is "when will they get here?" A live arrival time, sent by message as the phlebotomist sets off, answers it before anyone calls. It also lets patients plan around the visit instead of waiting at the door from the start of the window.

    At the door: label, record, hand off

    The visit itself is where home collection is most different from a walk-in draw, and where samples are most at risk.

    1. Confirm identity and preparation. Check the patient's name and date of birth, and ask whether they followed the preparation. Record it if they did not.
    2. Label at the doorstep. Label each tube in front of the patient, from the booking, so identification never depends on memory later.
    3. Record the collection. A scan or a tap on the phlebotomist's phone records the time the sample was drawn, which starts the turnaround clock.
    4. Pack for the journey. Keep tubes upright, at the right temperature, and protected from light where the test requires it.

    From doorstep to bench

    A home sample that waits in a car for hours can be technically acceptable and still make the report late. Plan routes so samples reach a lab or a collection hub within the stability window of the tests they carry, and scan them on arrival so the lab can see transport time as its own number.

    If a route regularly delivers samples late in the morning, the fix is often a handoff, not a faster driver: a rider who collects from phlebotomists mid-route, or a collection center that receives and racks home samples close to where they were drawn.

    After the visit

    Trust is finished, or lost, when the report arrives. Send the signed report to the patient and the referring doctor as soon as it is released, and let the patient know it is ready. If a sample has to be redrawn, call the patient yourself before they hear it from anyone else, and book the new visit on the same call.
