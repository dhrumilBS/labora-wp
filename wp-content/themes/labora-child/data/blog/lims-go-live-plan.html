    Why 30 days is enough

    Most labs that move off paper registers and spreadsheets do not struggle with the software. They struggle with the length of the project. When a rollout stretches over months, staff keep two ways of working, nobody trusts either, and the old habits win.

    A short, fixed plan avoids that. Thirty days is long enough to clean your data, train every role, and run a short parallel period. It is short enough that everyone remembers why you started. The plan below assumes one center goes live first; other centers follow the same plan, usually faster.

    If you run several centers, pick the one with the most typical day: not your busiest site, and not your smallest. What you learn there is what you repeat everywhere else.

    Paper, spreadsheets, or a LIMS

    Before planning the move, it helps to be clear about what changes. Many labs already use a mix: a paper register at the front desk, spreadsheets for billing and doctor referrals, and a word processor for reports.

    Week 1: clean your data before you import it

    Whatever is in your current test list arrives in the new system. Duplicates, old prices, and tests nobody has ordered in two years make every screen slower to use. Spend this week deciding what stays.

    • Export your test menu and remove tests not ordered in the last 12 months.
    • Agree on one name per test, and the sample type and container for each.
    • Update price lists, including any center-specific or panel prices.
    • List referring doctors with one spelling each, so referral reports add up.
    • Collect your current report formats, one per department.
    If the same test appears as "CBC", "Complete blood count" and "Haemogram", choose one now. Renaming after go-live means old and new reports no longer match in your numbers.

    Week 2: set up roles, barcodes, and reports

    Set up the system the way your lab actually works, not the way a demo works. Give each person a role with the access they need: front desk, phlebotomy, technicians, reporting doctors, and managers.

    Roles decide what each person sees and can change. Set them before training, so people learn their own screens.
    1. Create the roles and add every staff member, each with their own login.
    2. Connect barcode printers at registration and test a label on every tube type you use.
    3. Build report templates for your top 20 tests first; they usually cover most of your daily volume.
    4. Set up digital signatures for each reporting doctor.
    5. Run one complete test case through every step, from registration to the delivered report.
    Shared accounts make the audit trail useless: you can no longer tell who validated a result or changed a report. Create named logins from day one.

    Week 3: train by role, then run in parallel

    Train people on their own tasks, at their own desks, with real cases. A front desk session should be about registering a walk-in patient, a home collection booking, and a corporate patient; not about every screen in the system.

    After training, run paper and the new system side by side for three to five working days. Register every patient in both. This is where you find the gaps: a test without a price, a template missing a field, a printer that jams on one label size.

    The front desk registers each patient in the new system first and copies the visit number onto the paper register. At the end of each day, the lab manager compares both lists and fixes anything missing in the system before the next morning.     Week 4: go live and stop paper

    Pick a go-live day early in the week, when volume is normal and the whole team is in. On that day, paper registration stops. Keep the old registers for reference, but do not write new entries in them.

    If paper stays "just in case", staff will use it whenever they are busy, and you will run two systems indefinitely. Announce the date at the start of week three.

    Mistakes that slow a go-live down

    • Importing the old test list as it is, duplicates included.
    • Training everyone on everything, instead of each role on its own tasks.
    • Going live on the busiest day of the week, or the day before a holiday.
    • Keeping paper registration open with no end date.
    • Measuring nothing before go-live, so nobody can say what improved.

    How to measure whether it worked

    Measure the same few numbers in the two weeks before go-live and the four weeks after. Keep the definitions the same, or the comparison means nothing.
