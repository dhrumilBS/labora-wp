    The cost of two systems

    Many diagnostic centers grew one department at a time. The lab came first, with its own system. Ultrasound and X-ray came later, often with a separate reporting tool, a separate patient list, and sometimes a separate bill. Each system works on its own. The trouble is everything between them.

    The front desk registers the same patient twice. A radiologist reporting an abdominal ultrasound cannot see the liver function tests drawn the same morning without asking for a printout. The patient receives two reports through two channels, and the referring doctor calls to ask whether the third is still coming.

    None of this shows up as a single failure. It shows up as minutes, every case, every day.

    What one patient case changes

    In a shared system, a patient's visit is one case. Lab tests and imaging studies are ordered on it, billed on it, and reported on it. That sounds like an administrative detail, but it changes the work of almost everyone involved.

    Patient registered once per department Radiologist asks for lab results by phone Two bills, two receipts Reports delivered separately, at different times One registration and one bill for the visit Lab results visible next to the imaging study One payment record for every test and study Every report delivered together when signed

    For reporting doctors: context at review

    A radiologist reporting a thyroid ultrasound reads it differently when the thyroid panel is on the same screen. A pathologist validating an unexpected result reads it differently when they can see the imaging finding that prompted it. In one system, that context is part of the case, not a phone call away.

    One case holds the patient's lab results and imaging reports, so each reporting doctor sees what the others found.

    It also removes a quiet source of errors: the result that was looked up in the other system, for the patient with a similar name, on the wrong day.

    Seeing related results does not change who is responsible for a report. Each report is still reviewed and signed by the doctor authorized for that test or modality.

    One review and sign-off flow

    When every report moves through the same steps, the whole center can manage them the same way:

    StepLab reportImaging report
    PrepareResults entered and validated by techniciansFindings written from a template for the study
    ReviewPathologist reviews flagged and abnormal resultsRadiologist reviews and completes the impression
    SignDigital signature by an authorized doctorDigital signature by an authorized doctor
    DeliverSent to patient and referring doctorSent with the lab report, on the same case

    Turnaround targets and alerts then cover both departments. A case waiting for an ultrasound report is as visible as one waiting for a culture result.

    A referring doctor does not think in departments. They think in patients.

    One delivery for patients and doctors

    For the patient, the difference is simple: their results arrive together, as soon as the last report is signed, through the same message or portal. For the referring doctor, every report for the visit is in one place, which is what they wanted from the start.

    Moving both departments to one system

    1. Start with registration and billing. One patient record and one bill bring the quickest relief to the front desk.
    2. Bring delivery together next. Sending all reports for a visit together is what patients and doctors notice first.
    3. Move templates modality by modality. Begin with your highest-volume studies, usually ultrasound, then X-ray, CT, and MRI.
    4. Agree on review and signing rules. Decide who signs what, and how urgent cases are prioritized, before go-live.
