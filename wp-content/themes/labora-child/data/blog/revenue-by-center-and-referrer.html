    Why a weekly look beats a monthly report

    Most lab owners see revenue as a monthly total. By the time it arrives, the month is over, and the reasons behind a good or bad number are hard to trace. A short weekly look at a handful of numbers catches changes while you can still act on them: a center that slowed down, a doctor who stopped referring, a test that suddenly costs more to run than it earns.

    The aim is not more reports. It is a habit: twenty minutes, the same numbers, and one decision at the end.

    Revenue by center and by day

    Two centers with the same monthly revenue can be very different businesses. One may be busy every morning and empty after noon; the other may depend on a single weekly corporate camp. Reading revenue by day of week shows you when to staff, when to run home collection, and when a center has room for more.

    Compare each center with itself first, week on week and against the same week last month, before you compare centers with each other. Local holidays, a new clinic nearby, or a road closure explain more changes than any dashboard.

    Referring doctors as an early warning

    For many diagnostic labs, a large share of cases comes from a small group of referring doctors. A doctor who sends fewer cases rarely says so. The numbers simply drift down over a few weeks, often after a late report or a missed call.

    Sort referring doctors by the change against their own average, not by this week's total. A long-time referrer whose cases fell by half matters more than a new one sending two.

    When you see a drop, the best response is usually a conversation, not a discount. Ask about turnaround, report delivery, and anything that went wrong recently. Often it is one bad week you can fix.

    Keep dues in view

    Unpaid balances from patients, corporate clients, and partner labs are easy to overlook, because the work is done and the case is closed. Review dues by age every week: under 30 days, 30 to 60, and over 60. The oldest group is where follow-up matters most, and where a pattern, such as one corporate client that always pays late, becomes visible.

    A 20-minute weekly review

    • Revenue and cases by center, against last week and the same week last month
    • Busiest and quietest days at each center
    • Referring doctors with the largest change against their average
    • Dues over 60 days, and who will follow up on each
    • One decision for the coming week, written down

    Over a few months, the decisions add up: a home collection slot added on a quiet afternoon, a call to a referrer before they leave, a corporate contract renegotiated. That is how a weekly look at five numbers turns into growth.
